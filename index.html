What is the S&P 500?

The S&P 500 is a stock market index that tracks about 500 of the largest publicly traded companies in the United States. People often use it as a snapshot of how the overall U.S. stock market is doing.

1

Market-cap weighted

Bigger companies count for more. A company worth $3 trillion moves the index far more than one worth $30 billion.

2

Built-in diversification

One investment gives you exposure to hundreds of companies across technology, healthcare, finance, energy, consumer goods and more.

3

Self-cleaning

A committee regularly updates the list. Companies that shrink drop out, and growing companies take their place.

4

Dividends included

Many S&P 500 companies pay dividends. Reinvesting them has historically been a big part of total returns.

5

Not guaranteed

The index has had years with losses of more than 30%. Long-term averages hide a lot of short-term ups and downs.

6

A benchmark

Most professional fund managers are compared against the S&P 500, and many fail to beat it over long periods.

How can you invest in it?

You can't buy an index directly, but you can buy funds that copy it. Here are the most common ways.

Index ETFs

Exchange-traded funds that track the S&P 500 trade like a stock during market hours. They usually have very low annual fees (expense ratios).

Index mutual funds

Similar to ETFs, but priced once a day. Many allow automatic monthly investments, which makes regular investing easy.

Retirement accounts

401(k) plans and IRAs often offer S&P 500 index funds. These accounts can come with tax advantages.

Dollar-cost averaging

Investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule, whatever the price. You automatically buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when they're high.

Key principles for index investors

Simple habits matter more than picking the perfect moment.

⏳ Time in the market

Compounding needs time. Increase "Years invested" in the calculator and watch how the growth line pulls away from your contributions.

💸 Keep fees low

A 1% fee sounds small, but over decades it can take a large share of your gains. Try changing the fee slider.

🧘 Stay calm in downturns

Selling during a crash locks in losses. Historically the index has recovered from every major decline, though sometimes it took years.

🔁 Be consistent

Automatic monthly investing takes emotion out of the decision and builds the habit.

🛟 Emergency fund first

Keep a few months of expenses in savings so you're never forced to sell investments at a bad time.

🌍 Know what you own

The S&P 500 is U.S. large companies only. Some investors add bonds or international stocks for balance.

Common questions

Quick answers to things beginners often ask.

How much money do I need to start?

Very little. Many brokers let you buy fractional shares of S&P 500 ETFs for just a few dollars.

Is it a good time to invest right now?

Nobody can reliably predict short-term moves. That's why many long-term investors use dollar-cost averaging instead of trying to time the market.

Can I lose money?

Yes. The value of your investment can fall, especially over short periods. The longer your time horizon, the more time you have to recover from drops.

What's the difference between an ETF and a mutual fund?

ETFs trade throughout the day like stocks; mutual funds trade once a day at the closing price. Both can track the S&P 500 at low cost.

What happens to dividends?

You can take them as cash or reinvest them automatically to buy more shares. Reinvesting helps compounding.

Glossary

Words you'll see often.

Index
A list of investments used to measure part of the market, like the S&P 500.
Index fund
A fund that buys everything in an index so its returns match the index.
ETF
Exchange-traded fund: a basket of investments that trades on an exchange like a stock.
Expense ratio
The yearly fee a fund charges, shown as a percentage of your investment.
Compound interest
Earning returns on your past returns, not just on the money you put in.
Dividend
A share of company profits paid to shareholders.
Bear market
A decline of 20% or more from a recent high.
Bull market
A long period of rising prices.
Inflation
The general rise in prices that reduces what each dollar can buy.
